1 · Why process models? (Slides 3–4)

To understand the role of quality assurance in software development, you must first understand how software development works.

Software development process = a method for developing software that organizes the effort into a number of separate tasks and steps. It lets many people build large systems in an organized, manageable and trackable way, retaining control.

Four fundamental process activities

All process models share these; they differ only in how the four are organized and interleaved.

ActivityMeaning
SpecificationDefine requirements, functionality and constraints
DevelopmentBuild the software to meet the specification
ValidationCheck it does what the customer wants
EvolutionEvolve to meet changing needs and expectations

💡 Analogy: four ingredients in every recipe (S-D-V-E); each model is a different way of cooking them: all in a row, in loops, around a risk spiral…

2 · The Waterfall Model (Slides 5–11)
The first process model introduced; the earliest SDLC approach. Sequential: each phase must be completed (and signed off) before the next begins; no overlap. Output of one phase = input of the next; progress flows steadily downwards like a waterfall.

How to read it: each box hands its deliverable down-and-right; no arrows back up (that is the weakness).

PhaseWhat happens
RequirementsUnderstand what must be designed: function, purpose; study and mark the specifications of input and output / final product.
System DesignStudy requirement specs, prepare the design; specify hardware & system requirements, define overall system architecture; the code to be written next is planned here.
ImplementationDevelop the system as small programs called units; each unit developed and tested = Unit Testing.
Integration & TestingUnits integrated into a system after unit tests; constant testing to find flaws so the client has no problems at installation.
DeploymentAfter functional and non-functional testing, deploy to the customer environment or release to market.
MaintenanceAfter installation: modify system/components to alter attributes or improve performance. Triggered by customer change requests or defects found in live use. Client gets regular maintenance and support.
✅ Advantages❌ Disadvantages
Schedule with deadlines per stage
Easy to understand, explain and use
Easy to manage: rigid; each phase has deliverables and a review process
Phases done one at a time, no overlap
Works well for smaller projects with well-understood requirements		Hard to estimate time and cost per phase
Once in testing, very hard to go back and change a poorly thought-out concept
Not good for complex and object-oriented projects
Not suitable when requirements are at moderate to high risk of changing
3 · The Prototyping Model (Slides 12–15)
Addresses the requirements difficulty by adding an iterative, by-example requirements stage. A prototype = a partial implementation of the system with all external interfaces presented. Users try it and give feedback; real requirements are gradually refined. The final prototype serves as an example of the intended system.
StepMeaning
Requirements gathering & analysisLike waterfall but less stringent: the prototype exposes inadequacies.
Quick designSimple approximate initial design, refined during prototype iteration.
Build prototypeQuickly “hack together” approximate implementation showing salient external features.
Customer evaluationUsers validate prototype, report inadequacies.
Design refinementRefine design in response to feedback.
Full-scale developmentRemaining stages of the traditional waterfall.
Disadvantages
1. Waste work: prototypes built with substandard quality controls (“thrown together”) to speed iteration; they must be discarded after prototyping even if they solve significant problems.
2. Incomplete prototypes: full prototypes of complex systems are hard/impossible to build quickly; done in parts, so they may miss critical requirements at integration.
4 · The Spiral Model (Slides 16–21)
Combines prototyping + waterfall; favoured for large, expensive, complicated projects. Supports risk handling; drawn as a spiral with many loops. A refinement of waterfall built on continuous documentation and evaluation of risk, from experience with waterfall on large projects.

Read it: each loop = one phase (layer) passing through the 4 quadrants; the radius grows with cost; repeat until the product is done.

For each layer

  1. Determine objectives: specific objectives; constraints on process and product; alternatives; potential risks of each alternative.
  2. Assess & reduce risks: detailed analysis of each risk; reduce it (e.g. create a prototype to check); choose alternatives that minimize risk.
  3. Develop & validate: choose/modify the development model from the risk analysis:
    • UI risks dominate → evolutionary prototyping
    • Safety risks major → formal methods
    • Integration problems → waterfall
  4. Review & plan: evaluate results; decide whether another layer is needed; plan next phase.
Disadvantages
• Heavyweight: lots of overhead (documentation, meetings) ⇒ slow; suited to large projects with long timelines.
• Not really a development model: a “meta-model”: describes how to carry out stages, not what the stages are. (Strength: finds problems early at every stage ⇒ high quality.)
• Depends on risk analysis: needs a very experienced team; high dependency on people quality (itself a risk!).
• Not for beginners: layers are flexible, not explicitly laid out; each layer’s goals and plan decided by the team.
5 · The Iterative Development Model (Slides 22–23)
Breaks development of a large application into smaller chunks; feature code is designed, developed and tested in repeated cycles.

The subset should: (1) satisfy immediate user needs; (2) serve as a vehicle of training for customers and learning for developers. Analysing the subset leads to changes to design and requirements, from which a new subset product is built. Over many iterations: a system meeting evolving needs with improved design.

Disadvantages
• Needs a small team: no large-scale parallel development; focuses on one remaining risk at a time; works best with relatively small teams.
• Needs early architecture: overall architecture must be designed early; hard to change later. If it can be settled early, very successful at producing high-quality products.

Smart Summary

  • Process model = organizes development into tasks and steps (organized, manageable, trackable).
    • All share 4 activities: Specification, Development, Validation, Evolution; differ in organization/interleaving.
  • Waterfall: first model; sequential, no overlap; 6 phases: Requirements → Design → Implementation (unit testing) → Integration & Testing → Deployment → Maintenance.
    • + simple, manageable, small/well-understood projects. − hard to change, hard estimates, not for complex/OO or changing requirements.
  • Prototyping: partial implementation with all external interfaces; iterate with user feedback; then waterfall stages.
    • − wasted work (substandard, discarded); incomplete prototypes miss requirements.
  • Spiral: prototyping + waterfall + risk handling; 4 steps per layer (Objectives → Assess risks → Develop & validate → Review & plan).
    • − heavyweight, meta-model, needs experienced team, not for beginners.
  • Iterative: small chunks, repeated cycles; subset trains users and developers.
    • − small team only, needs early architecture.

Memory Optimization

Mnemonics

S-D-V-E (4 activities): “Software Developers Value Evolution.”

Waterfall phases R-D-I-I-D-M: “Really Deep Ideas Improve Delivered Machines” (Requirements, Design, Implementation, Integration & testing, Deployment, Maintenance).

Spiral O-A-D-R: “Objectives, Assess, Develop, Review” → “Our App Deserves Risk-checks.”

Spiral choice: U-I → prototype · S-afety → formal methods · I-ntegration → waterfall (“UI-Safe-Int: Pro-Formal-Fall”).

Common mistakes & traps

  • Waterfall is not iterative and has no overlap of phases.
  • Unit testing happens in Implementation; system testing in Integration & Testing.
  • Prototype = partial implementation, all external interfaces; it is usually discarded.
  • Spiral is a meta-model; it does not name the stages.
  • Spiral for large, expensive projects, not beginners or small ones.
  • Iterative’s weakness: team size and early architecture.
  • Maintenance is triggered by change requests or defects in live use.
  • Waterfall is the earliest/first process model.
